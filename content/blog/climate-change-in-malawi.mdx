In Malawi, climate change is not a distant projection. It is the rain that arrives weeks late, the maize that withers at tasselling stage, the river that takes a bridge in a single night. Ask a farming family in Zomba, Nsanje, or Mzimba what has changed, and you will not hear jargon about pathways and scenarios. You will hear about seasons that no longer behave the way grandparents remember.

This note tries to take that lived experience seriously — to describe how climate pressure shows up in daily life, what communities are already doing about it, and what outside organisations like ours must learn before offering anything.

## A country exposed on several fronts

Malawi's geography concentrates risk. A long, narrow country stretching along the Rift Valley, it combines highland plateaus, the vast Lake Malawi system, and the low-lying Shire Valley in the south. Each zone has its own hazards, but they rhyme: water arriving at the wrong time, in the wrong amount.

The southern districts sit in the path of storms that form in the Indian Ocean and travel through Mozambique. When those systems make landfall, the Shire River and its tributaries swell rapidly. Homes built of earth brick dissolve. Fields planted weeks earlier disappear under silt. Roads that connect villages to clinics and markets are cut for days.

In the central region and parts of the north, the signature hazard is different: the mid-season dry spell. Rains begin, farmers plant, and then the sky closes for stretches that arrive exactly when crops need moisture most. A season can look promising in December and be lost by February — not through one dramatic event, but through absence.

Underneath both patterns sits a slower shift. Lake levels fluctuate. Wells that once served through the dry season now run low earlier. Firewood collection takes longer as nearby woodlots thin. None of these changes announces itself as "climate change" in the moment. Together, they tighten the margins within which households already operate.

## Rain-fed farming means the sky is the economy

Most rural households in Malawi farm, and most farming depends directly on rainfall. There is no large-scale irrigation to fall back on for the majority of smallholders. Planting is a bet placed once a year: on timing, on seed choice, on whether the rains will hold.

That makes predictability itself a form of wealth. When the onset of rains becomes harder to read — when elders' indicators no longer align with what the sky does — the whole calendar slips. Plant too early and seed rots or germinates then dies. Plant too late and the crop runs out of season before maturing.

Farmers describe adapting in real time. Some split planting across dates to hedge the bet. Some intercrop maize with legumes so that if one crop struggles, another may hold. Some save seed from plants that survived a hard year rather than buying unfamiliar varieties. These are not "coping mechanisms" in the passive sense that reports sometimes imply. They are active experiments, evaluated harvest by harvest.

> The most sophisticated climate adaptation research in Malawi is happening without funding, without publications, in fields where a failed experiment means hunger.

Any serious climate organisation has to begin from that recognition.

## Cyclones, floods, and the long recovery

Flood events get the headlines, but recovery is where the real story lives. After waters recede, households face layered losses: the standing crop, stored grain contaminated by water, goats and chickens swept away, the house itself needing repair before the next rains.

Replanting after a flood is constrained by everything at once — seed availability, labour, debts incurred during displacement. Families that host displaced relatives absorb extra mouths without extra income. Children miss school weeks. Clinics see the familiar post-flood wave of waterborne illness.

What prolongs recovery is rarely a single missing input. It is the stacking: transport costs rise exactly when households have least to sell, building materials become scarce exactly when everyone needs them, and the next planting window does not wait for anyone to be ready.

This is why communities consistently prioritise resilience over relief in conversation, even while needing relief in the moment. Relief addresses the event. Resilience addresses the stacking — savings groups that survive displacement, seed systems that function after a bad year, housing techniques that fail less catastrophically.

## Food, water, and energy are one conversation

In policy documents these are separate sectors. In a Malawian village they are the same day.

Consider a dry-season morning. A woman walks further for water because the nearer borehole is strained. Firewood takes longer to gather, so cooking starts later and ends later. Less water and less fuel narrow what can be cooked, which narrows nutrition — particularly for young children. Time spent fetching water and wood is time not spent on the vegetable garden, on trading, on rest.

Energy pressure and landscape pressure feed each other. As wood becomes scarce, households turn to crop residues or lower-quality fuels, with consequences for indoor air and for soil fertility that loses organic matter. As trees thin on surrounding hills, runoff accelerates, which worsens both flood peaks and dry-season water availability.

Breaking these loops requires interventions that respect how intertwined they are. A fuel-efficient stove matters more when paired with a woodlot the community actually controls. A borehole matters more when its maintenance committee has real authority and spare parts are obtainable. An irrigation scheme matters more when the water users' association, not an outside office, sets the schedule.

## Who carries the heaviest load

Climate pressure is not distributed evenly within communities, and Malawians will tell you exactly who absorbs most of it.

Women manage water, food preparation, and much of farm labour, so every tightening of those systems lands first on their time and health. When a harvest fails, it is typically women who eat last and least. When water points fail, it is girls whose school attendance drops because mornings stretch longer.

Young people face a different bind. Farming is harder to make pay under volatile rains, yet alternatives — trading, casual labour, migration to towns or across borders — carry their own risks. Climate stress becomes one more push factor in decisions that were already difficult.

Elders, meanwhile, hold knowledge that is both invaluable and under strain: reading clouds, soils, and seasons in ways no satellite can replicate, while watching some of those readings become less reliable. The task is not to romanticise that knowledge or discard it, but to create spaces where it meets new information on equal terms.

## What communities are already doing

Spend time in villages during a difficult season and a pattern emerges. People are not waiting for rescue. They are organising.

Village savings and loan groups pool small contributions so members can borrow for seed, school fees, or emergency food without turning to ruinous terms. Farmer groups multiply seed of varieties that performed locally. Communities maintain riverbank plantings, contour ridges, and mulching practices that hold soil and moisture. After floods, neighbours rebuild houses together in rotations that no project designed.

None of this is labelled "adaptation programming." All of it is adaptation.

The honest question for an organisation like Carbon8 is whether outside support strengthens these systems or bypasses them. History in this region offers cautionary examples: inputs distributed without local seed systems, infrastructure built without maintenance ownership, trainings delivered without follow-through. Communities remember every one of them.

## What outside actors routinely get wrong

Three mistakes recur often enough to name.

First, arriving with answers. A project designed elsewhere lands with fixed activities and fixed timelines, then seeks "community participation" in something already decided. Participation becomes attendance. Ownership never forms, and everyone can predict the ending.

Second, measuring the wrong things. Counting seedlings distributed says nothing about survival after two dry seasons. Counting training attendees says nothing about changed practice. Communities notice when reporting needs of distant funders drive what gets counted, and they adjust their engagement accordingly.

Third, promising scale prematurely. A pilot that worked in one village under particular leadership is declared a model for a district. The social conditions that made it work — trust, follow-through, local legitimacy — cannot be photocopied, and the replication fails in ways that discredit the original idea.

> We would rather document ten honest lessons from small work than claim one large success we cannot evidence.

## Our learning agenda

Carbon8 is at concept stage. We do not operate field programmes yet, and this note claims no results. What we can state is the set of questions guiding us:

- How do communities in different agro-ecological zones describe their priority risks, in their own terms?
- Which existing groups — savings groups, farmer clubs, youth associations — already function as resilience infrastructure, and what do they say they need?
- What kinds of support (information, seed diversity, small-scale water management, savings protection) strengthen self-organisation rather than replacing it?
- How should any future work be documented so that communities, not just funders, can judge whether it helped?

Answering those questions requires time in villages, fluency in local languages, and the discipline to write down what we hear accurately — including what contradicts our assumptions.

## Where this goes

Malawi contributes little to the emissions driving global warming, yet absorbs consequences shaped elsewhere. That injustice frames everything, but it cannot be the whole story. The other half is agency: the skill, organisation, and persistence with which Malawian communities already respond.

Our conviction is that climate finance and climate technology should flow toward that agency — patiently, transparently, and on terms communities set. Whether Carbon8 earns a role in that flow depends on whether we listen well enough, document honestly enough, and stay small enough for long enough to learn.

The rains will come again in a few months. In villages across the country, planting decisions are already being weighed. The least we can do is pay attention, and the most we can promise is to report what we learn without embellishment.
